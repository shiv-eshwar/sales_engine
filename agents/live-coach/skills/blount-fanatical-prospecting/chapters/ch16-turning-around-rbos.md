# Chapter 16: Turning Around RBOs: Reflex Responses, Brush-Offs, and Objections

## Core Idea
The rubber meets the road at rejection. **RBOs** — reflex responses, brush-offs, and objections — feel personal because they *are* personal to your brain; “let it roll off your back” is a psychopath standard, not a sales one. Do not **overcome** (argue them into being wrong). **Disrupt** expectations with verbal judo: **Anchor. Disrupt. Ask.** After two RBOs, graciously leave and return another day. When the horse is dead, dismount: **NEXT**.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **RBO taxonomy**:
  - **Reflex response**: Automatic buyer script, often untrue, no conscious deceit — “not interested,” “we’re happy/all set,” “I’m busy/in a meeting/running out the door,” “just looking” (inbound). Like saying “just looking” in a store when you actually need the cord.
  - **Brush-off**: Conflict avoidance, often a polite lie — “call me later,” “get back in a month,” **“just send some information”** (greatest brush-off of all time). Prospects lie because sellers trained them to: truth gets them questioned, bullied, made to feel dumb (Godin).
  - **Objection**: Rarer, more truthful, usually with a **because** — new competitor contract, big project, out of town, budgets locked. Opens a real turnaround or a qualify-for-later shift.

- **Finite no-set**: Not interested; no budget; too busy; send info; overwhelmed; just looking. Wording varies (“competitor does a good job” = happy). Pattern-match; don’t treat each as unique.

- **Three-Step Turnaround (Anchor → Disrupt → Ask)** — practiced scripts so intonation is relaxed and the mind stays on the prospect, not word-search:
  1. **Anchor (ledge)**: Millisecond for neocortex to catch amygdala (bear vs. no). A short holding line so fight-or-flight doesn’t wreck the call.
  2. **Disrupt**: Pull, don’t push. Unexpected agree/takeaway so they lean in. Avoid salesperson-only filler especially **“I understand”** (insincere, zero empathy, pitch incoming).
  3. **Ask**: Assumptive specific time or information immediately after the disrupt. ~Half the time a second, truer RBO appears — handle once more. **Never fight.** Two RBOs and no turn → move on, come back later.

- **Why not “overcome”**: Overcome = defeat an opponent. You cannot argue someone into believing they are wrong; push makes heels dig in. Even accidental yeses happen *despite* the fight. Creates animosity.

- **When the horse is dead, dismount / NEXT**: Rudeness, slams, “never call again,” personal shots. They often forget you by the next minute (Tuesday scream, Wednesday best-friend). Kids get back on the horse. Anger is energy — convert to determination; achievement is revenge. Index card: **NEXT**. If they said never call, he still calls. Don’t dwell on the one ugly touch among thousands.

## Key Concepts
- **Vulnerability** (Brown): uncertainty + risk + emotional exposure. Loss of control is the awful feeling.
- **Scripts for RBOs, not for the whole pitch**: Politicians off-teleprompter stumble; rehearse until the turnaround is *your* voice. Analyze what already works; recorder/role-play/coach.
- **Verbal judo at 100 mph**: Nimble, no injury, commitment of time or information.
- **Prospects trained us**: Accept brush-offs because they feel less like rejection and sellers also hate conflict.

## Mental Models
- Use **Anchor** the instant your gut punches — buy the brain a beat.
- Use **agree/takeaway/specify** as disruptors: happy → “then don’t change”; busy → “I figured”; send info → “tell me specifically what.”
- Use **two-RBO rule** to protect Golden Hours from dead-horse debates.
- Use **NEXT** when rumination starts — they moved on; you must too.

## Anti-patterns
- **Winging unique debates** on a finite set of nos.
- **Arguing “you’re wrong / I can make you happier.”**
- **“I understand…”** then pitch.
- **Accepting “send info”** as a win (it’s a brush-off).
- **Fighting past two RBOs**.
- **Logging NEVER CALL** and replaying the insult for years in boot camp.
- **Pretending rejection isn’t personal** so you never build a control framework.

## Worked Example
**Busy:** “Look, I’m busy.”  
Anchor: “Nancy, that’s exactly why I called.”  
Disrupt: “I figured you would be, so I want a time that’s more convenient.”  
Ask: “How about next Wednesday at 3 PM instead?”

**Not interested:** Anchor: “That’s what a lot of my current clients said the first time I called.”  
Disrupt: “Most people aren’t interested before they see how much I can save them. I don’t know if we’re a fit, but doesn’t it make sense to find out in a short meeting?”  
Ask: “Friday at 2 PM?”

**Happy with provider:** Anchor: “That’s fantastic!”  
Disrupt: “Anytime you’re getting great rates and service, you shouldn’t think about changing. I just want to get to know you. Even if we never do business, a competitive quote helps you keep those guys honest.”  
Ask: “Tuesday 11:30 AM?”

**Send information:** Disrupt by calling the bluff: “Tell me specifically what you’re looking for.” Forces engagement or reveals the lie.

**Dead horse:** Prospect screams never call. Impulse: CRM death-note, peer therapy, revenge fantasy. Protocol: NEXT. Call someone else in the same block. Optionally call this person another day — they often won’t remember Tuesday.

## Key Takeaways
1. Rejection feels personal; manage it with a framework, don’t deny it.
2. Sort RBO: reflex vs brush-off vs real objection-with-because.
3. Turn around, don’t overcome: Anchor, Disrupt, Ask.
4. Rehearse a small script library for the finite no-set until it sounds like you.
5. Never say “I understand”; don’t fight; two RBOs then leave.
6. “Send information” is usually a brush-off — specify or dismount.
7. NEXT: convert anger into the next dial; they already forgot you.

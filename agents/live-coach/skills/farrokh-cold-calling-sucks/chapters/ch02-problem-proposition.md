# Chapter 2: The Problem Proposition

## Core Idea
Dump the value proposition. If you describe the problem in triggering, movie-scene detail, you barely need to talk about the product — one sentence that makes the problem go away is enough, then validate interest before asking for a meeting.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Problem Proposition**: A problem so specific the prospect only needs one sentence that removes it.
  - When to use: Immediately after the opener, in the first 60 seconds.
  - How: (1) Triggering Problem → (2) One-Sentence Solution → (3) Interest-Based CTA.
  - Why it works: Gong product-description data — problem language ~16% success vs social proof ~12% vs buzzwords ~5.5% (problem ~3x buzzwords). People act on pain more than benefits; value has no meaning without a problem.
  - Failure mode: Fuzzy “save time / make money / mitigate risk” — every product claims those.

- **Triggering Problem (Step 1)**: Repaint a movie scene, not a slogan.
  - When to use: Always. This is 80% of the prop.
  - How: Stack **persona** (who felt it), **annoyances** (what stung), **scenery** (when/where), **emotion** (how they’d name the feeling). Steal customer language from call recordings and CS, not marketing copy.
  - Persona split: **At the power line** (day-to-day grind), **above the power line** (money/risk the exec feels), **another department** (second-order impact). Same product, different movie.

- **One-Sentence Solution (Step 2)**: “We do X so that the problem goes away,” where X is the single biggest differentiator that *allows* you to solve it.
  - When to use: After the problem lands. Brush over it; do not orate.
  - How: Minimum detail to be believable, not so much you spawn objections. Do not list every feature. Social proof fills gaps.
  - Failure mode: Too little (“we save you on taxes” = guru ad). Too much (mechanism they already hate, e.g. “via life insurance”).

- **Interest-Based CTA (Step 3)**: Validate interest before calendaring.
  - When to use: After the one-sentence solution, every time.
  - How: One decision only. Soften (“open to,” “moderately interesting”), prefer **no-based questions** (“opposed to taking a peek?”), add a **mini push-away** (“my guess is you’re all set”).
  - Then book: Suggest **time ranges** not exact slots; schedule within 1–2 weeks (show rate decays); confirm email live; ask them to **accept** the invite; if they want to “email times,” **throw a dart at the calendar** plus backups — do not reopen the decision to meet.

## Key Concepts
- **Value proposition (banned)**: Buzzword billboard — “all-in-one,” “single source of truth,” “leading provider.” Triggers telemarketer alarms.
- **Categorizations (banned)**: Never name the category (“we’re a CRM / tax software / realtor”). If they have one, they dismiss you. Stay on the problem.
- **Random accolades (banned)**: Years in business, awards — only if they enable the fix.
- **One decision at a time**: Heavy CTAs force four yeses at once (care? believe? meet this stranger? free Wed 10?). They say no to all.
- **Throw a dart**: Send a placeholder invite + backups instead of email ping-pong.
- **80/20 problem-to-product**: Spend ~80% on the problem.

## Mental Models
- Use a **Problem Proposition** whenever you would have “pitched.”
- Think of the CTA as **walking past a shop**: “Wanna peek just for kicks?” — shrug, don’t hard-close.
- Join the **ridiculousness of the problem** in tone; monotone empathy is fake.
- Brush the solution, then **push away**. Pressure belongs nowhere in minute one.

## Anti-patterns
- **Billy Mays pitch**: Features, categories, awards, “#1 in volume.”
- **Calendar-first CTA**: “Wed 10 or 2 for a 30-min demo?” after a cold open.
- **Assuming the email**: Invite bounces; meeting dies.
- **Email-only scheduling**: Ghost magnet. Dart + backups.
- **Same problem for every persona**: CFO ≠ billing manager ≠ partner.

## Worked Example
*Billing software to a law-firm CFO after Tailored Permission (reconstructed)*

You: “Typically when I’m talking to CFOs after a case win, they’re drowning in carrier bill rules — one unapproved word and the whole invoice gets rejected, so someone is sitting there rewriting bills instead of collecting.
We flag those violations as attorneys enter time so the write-offs stop.
My guess is you’re all set. Open to taking a peek so you at least know what’s out there?”
Prospect: “Yeah, sure.”
You: “I know I interrupted you — and I need to jump too. Tuesday 9–11 Pacific or Thursday 2–4? I’ll send the invite now; mind accepting it when it hits so it actually lands on your calendar?”

If they stall on times: “I’ll throw a dart for later this week plus backups. Accept if it works, or pick a backup — either way we’re on.”

## Key Takeaways
1. Replace value props with a triggering problem in the prospect’s language.
2. One sentence: we do X so the problem goes away — X is the differentiator, not the category.
3. Validate interest with softeners, no-based asks, and mini push-aways before any calendar.
4. Book with ranges, 1–2 week horizon, live email confirm, explicit accept, dart-if-needed.
5. Tone: inhabit the problem, shrug the CTA, do not orate the product.
6. Build the script, then run it 100 times off-phone like the opener.

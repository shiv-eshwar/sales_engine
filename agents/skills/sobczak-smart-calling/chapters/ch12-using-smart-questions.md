# Chapter 12: Using Smart Questions

## Core Idea
After the opening, do not present. Questions uncover possible needs, help *them* feel those needs, and capture their words for the later recommendation. A benefit is only a benefit if they perceive it at that instant — so convert benefits into questions.

## Frameworks Introduced (When / How)
- **Possible-benefit → question line**: Use instead of reciting a benefit sheet.
  1. Describe a possible benefit.
  2. Describe under what circumstances it would be a *true* benefit.
  3. Draft questions that uncover those circumstances (broad first, then specific). Social engineering may already have the answers; still ask so they say it.

- **Assumptive problem questions**: Use when you reasonably believe the pain exists.
  - How: Assume the problem; ask them to visualize and describe it. Not “any problems / needs / are you satisfied?” — those are vague and make them do too much work.
    - Not “need temp help?” → “How often is the department staring at a deadline with more work than time?”
    - Not “happy with advertising?” → “When have you paid for promotions that didn’t bring the calls you expected?”
  - Stems: What happens when / How do you handle it when / What do you do when / Tell me about situations where / Implications of / How does it affect / Is it a challenge to / Are you frustrated because / Are you embarrassed because.

- **Loaded benefit question**: Use to imply a third-party problem you solve.
  - How: “Most customers found they couldn’t get deliveries within five days with the other service. What’s your experience?” Paints inconvenience without accusing them.

- **Iceberg theory of questioning**: Use after every first answer.
  - How: The first answer is the tip. People buy because of the reasons *under* the water. Pause, then next questions: why, how much, tell me more, what else. Do not stop at the first yes/no.

- **Clarify fuzzy phrases**: Use when you hear someday-speak.
  - How: “Let’s stay in touch” → “So you plan to move forward? When?” “We’ll consider it” → “Which aspects will you weight most?” “I’ll look it over” → “On what criteria?” “I’ll bounce it around” → “Does that mean you personally are sold?”

## Key Concepts
- **Lead them to water; help them realize they are thirsty** — do not force the drink.
- **Quality of question = quality of answer**: Not “does that happen a lot?” → “How often in the past three months?” / “What was the impact in increased sales?”
- **Don’t ask what they like best about the current supplier** — you may hear a eulogy. Ask selection criteria, determining factors, eval process, what they do when a vendor fails.
- **Ask about them**: “What are your responsibilities?” (Meisenheimer). Favorite subject is them.
- **Benefit questions vs. inane leading questions**: Don’t “you’d like to save money, wouldn’t you?” That’s cornering. Do: peer result + “is that something you’d like to learn more about?”
- **Economize**: Don’t “Is there someone who handles that?” “Yes.” “Who?” → “Who handles that?”
- **Did-you-know / are-you-aware** are presentations in disguise. Ask what they require instead.
- **Decision-process questions** (soften): “What route does this decision take?” “What needs to happen on your end?”
- **Annoyance mining**: Unnecessary work, tasks they shouldn’t have to do.
- **Dollarize** (Fox/Gregory direction): Attach numbers to pain when they will.

## Mental Models
- Use questions to **move them into feeling the problem**, not to audition your catalog.
- Think of the first answer as **the visible iceberg**.
- Prefer **assumptive scenes** over abstract “any issues?”
- When they speak in fog, **force a clock, a criterion, or a personal yes.**

## Anti-patterns
- **Presenting at the question phase** — creates objections.
- **Satisfaction / needs / problems** as naked nouns.
- **Two questions where one works.**
- **Inane tag questions** that make them feel stupid if they say no.
- **Stopping at the tip** of the iceberg.
- **“Did you know we offer six printer lines?”** before you know they care about printers.

## Worked Example
Distributor PVP: national warehouses, next-day without extra freight, deep stock so dealers need not inventory slow movers.

Circumstances: they have paid rush freight; they have lost sales when a part wasn’t on the shelf.

Questions: “Tell me about the plumbing department and what you stock.” Then who they buy from, order pattern, inventory. Then assumptive: “What do you do when a customer needs something tomorrow that you don’t have?” “Tell me about times you paid extra for rush delivery to get a part for a customer.”

If a counter clerk already described those scenes (Ch 4), you still ask so the decision maker paints the picture in their own words — those words return in Ch 14.

## Key Takeaways
1. Convert every possible benefit into a circumstance-based question line.
2. Use assumptive problem and loaded-benefit questions; skip vague “any needs?”
3. Practice iceberg: first answer is not the reason they buy.
4. Clarify fuzzy time and commitment language on the spot.
5. Ask criteria and failure paths, not “what do you love about the incumbent?”
6. One precise question beats a chain of closed warm-ups.
7. Capture their exact words; you will recommend in that language.

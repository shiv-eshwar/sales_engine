# Chapter 2: Creating Your Possible Value Proposition

## Core Idea
Prospects do not care about your quota, product, or slogan. They buy for their reasons. Your job before the call is to write Possible Value Propositions (PVPs) — outcome language you will test, not assume, on the call.

## Frameworks Introduced (When / How)
- **Possible Value Proposition (PVP)**: Use whenever you write a voicemail, screener answer, opening, or need-uncovering question.
  - How: Describe the *possible* result of owning/using what you sell — in the buyer’s world — then confirm it with questions. Keep the word **possible** so you do not pitch as if value is already proven for *this* person.
  - Why it works: Value is the outcome in a photo with them in it, not a feature of the product. “Possible” stops presumptuous pitching.
  - Failure mode: Reciting marketing “benefits” that are slogans. The buyer thinks “so what?”

- **All-about-them mind-set**: Use from the first minute of prep.
  - How: Everything you think, do, and say is about the prospect. Two fundamentals: people love to buy but hate to be sold; people buy for their reasons, not yours.

- **Pain and gain inventory**: Use to draft PVP phrases.
  - How: For each buyer type, write what they want (gain) and what they want to avoid (pain). Combine into one line when useful (“we fix failed inspections so landlords pass”). Pain/loss often moves people more than gain.

- **Role-specific PVP**: Use when multiple titles touch the deal.
  - How: Write different possible value for executive assistants, users, user-managers, and top executives. Same company does not mean same value.

## Key Concepts
- **You are not your customer**: Your cool product and marketing’s reasons do not matter until the buyer perceives them.
- **Find a need and fill it**: Opposite of “we built it because we think it’s great.”
- **Personal job motivators**: Looking good to the boss, not blowing a high-profile project, hitting numbers, peer respect, less workload.
- **Sensory, specific wants**: Not “good customer service” — “first-call tech support with no hold.”
- **Easy-way PVP**: Cut time, hassle, dread, stress. Fill-ins: easier to __; less time to __; eliminate the task of __.
- **Cost-cut PVP**: Cut, trim, control, prevent increases, consolidate, take credits — always attached to a specific cost category.
- **Differential advantage**: What sets you apart that the buyer can feel (on-time guarantee, one-hour delivery vs. leaving the job site).
- **Results already delivered**: Tangible, numbered outcomes from your best and longest customers. Ask customers if you do not know.
- **Inadequacy motivator**: Status, losing RFPs, negative reviews, unconfident prospecting — if you fix a felt deficiency, name the result.
- **Free is not a benefit**: If the deliverable is unwanted, “free evaluation” still dies.

## Mental Models
- Use the **group-photo test**: In any photo, people look at themselves first. Your copy must put them in the picture.
- Think of a **benefit** as valuable only if this person perceives it *at this instant*. Yesterday’s benefit can be meaningless today.
- Prefer **outcome language** over product names: recruiting cost down and retention up, not “health insurance.”
- Use **Who am I talking to, and what might they want?** as the pre-call question instead of “How do I sell?”

## Anti-patterns
- **Motto openings**: “We Unfold the Technology of Tomorrow, Today.” “Practically Perfect Vinyl Siding.” “Cost-effective solutions.” “Most respected.” “In business 40 years.” “National with 30 locations.”
- **Name-dropping the wrong proof**: Citing Chase/BofA to a community bank can prove you are too big.
- **Pitching “free” as the hook**: Free is irrelevant if they do not want the thing.
- **One PVP for every title**: Purchasing, users, and the corner office do not value the same outcome. Lower-level “efficiency” talk can sound like job elimination.
- **Unique value proposition as certainty**: Sobczak uses *possible* so you still ask.

## Worked Example
Weak: “Hi, we sell drywall supplies. I’d like to tell you what we sell and see if we could become a supplier.”

PVP-built reconstruction: After learning from Jean that installers leave jobs to pick up materials: “Jean mentioned installers spend a lot of time leaving jobs to run to your supplier. What sets us apart is trucks that deliver within an hour when contractors need something. I’d like to ask a few questions to see if that might be of value.”

Hero PVP (meeting planners): After a client said the program made her look like a hero, the caller uses that outcome with other planners: other planners say these programs help them look like a hero to their clients. Then ask: “What do you want your clients to tell you after a meeting?”

Numbered proof PVP: “We’ve helped pediatric dentists cut new-patient acquisition cost about 50 percent while increasing new patients about 25 percent within six months.” That sentence can live in voicemail, opening, and questions — still *possible* until this dentist confirms the goal.

## Key Takeaways
1. Write PVPs in outcome language before you dial; use them in voicemail, with screeners, and in openings.
2. Keep “possible” until questions confirm value for this buyer, this moment.
3. Inventory wants and avoids by buyer role; get sensory and specific.
4. Strengthen PVPs with ease, cost-cut, differentiation, numbered customer results, and inadequacy-fixes.
5. Match PVP to title: assistants protect time; users care about daily work; executives care about ROI.
6. Kill slogans that fail the “what does this do for me?” test.
7. If you do not know why customers bought, call them and ask for numbered results.

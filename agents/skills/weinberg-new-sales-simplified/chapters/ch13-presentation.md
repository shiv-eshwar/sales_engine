# Chapter 13: I Thought I Was Supposed to Make a Presentation

## Core Idea
Presenting is not selling. By **sales law, a first meeting shall never be a presentation**. Discovery must precede presentation; if you cannot fill a slide with *their* situation, you have no business pitching—even if they asked. When you cannot get a pre-meeting, convert the first chunk of the “pitch” into discovery. Break the mold; compliance with the buyer’s theater is often the riskier path.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Sales law: first meeting ≠ presentation. Ever.** When they ask for a capabilities overview, insist on process: you deliver maximum value only after several steps. Assert; don’t default.
- **Four-slide customer-first open** (then convert monologue → dialogue):
  1. Title
  2. Suggested agenda
  3. “Companies turn to us when…” (3–5 Power Statement issues)
  4. “Our understanding of your situation…” (discovery receipts)
  After slide 4: **stop**. Ask where you nailed it, what you missed; have the senior person rank/prioritize; confirm assumptions; salt wounds if they allow. Then tailor the rest.
- **Insist on a meeting before the pitch**: no slide-4 facts → no presentation. “If you want our best, help us earn it.” Hard in procurement-gated worlds; still the standard.
- **When they will not meet first** (RFP silence, agency “pitch” culture): run an abbreviated Ch 11 inside the hour. Calmly: we brought four hours of work; to make 60 minutes useful, we need 15 on why you invited us, threats/opportunities, (optional) prior-agency experience; then we choose live what to show. CEO gives a 3-minute why-clients-pick-us; then questions; then customized stories. That is selling, not presenting.
- **Break the mold (Jerry the kitchen guy)**: three remodelers bring binders and graph paper. Jerry opens pantries, asks about kids and a future dog, questions a weird soffit, cuts a hole (after confirming they will actually do the project) to see what is inside—because he cannot specify a solution without discovery. Then: “What is your vision for this room?” He wins on the spot. Discovery made visible.

## Key Concepts
- **Presentation / “preezentation”**: visceral fail-word; slots with his other hated words because it flips dialogue into lecture.
- **Tee up the demo**: partner-speak for a self-focused slide dump.
- **Spray and pray / dog and pony / capabilities overview**.
- **Never cede process**: even if the partner “owns” the account and is President’s Club. Trust the sales gut when it feels wrong.
- **Even when presenting, interrogate the beginning**: why us, why this committee, what issues, what does success look like, what do you want from today?

## Mental Models
- Use **slide 4 as a go/no-go**: cannot write it → should not stand up.
- Prefer **live customization** over a perfect generic deck.
- Think **Jerry’s soffit**: if you don’t look inside, you are guessing.
- Treat **rule-following sameness** as the dangerous strategy in a four-way pitch.

## Anti-patterns
- Frank pattern: buildings, logos, process flowcharts; zero questions; president has 30 minutes; demo requested at minute 22; committee says it wasn’t what they needed. (They later won on relationship + product anyway—does not excuse the wreck.)
- Going along as the respectful newbie.
- Caring more about the internet connection than connecting with people.
- Blind agency pitches: “that’s just how it’s done.”
- RFPs that forbid discovery—and sellers who don’t even try to convert the room.

## Worked Example
**Frank reconstruction (decision lessons, not a transcript):** Channel partner star picks up two LMS sellers at Newark. Plan: he tees up, they demo. Boardroom, division president: “I have 30 minutes.” Nine-ish self slides (campus photos, logos, flowcharts), no questions. President interrupts for the demo. Afterward, “Was this on target?” No. Lessons logged: never cede process; if it feels wrong, speak; first meeting cannot be a presentation; turn requested presentations into dialogues from the first minutes.

**Counterfactual open:** easel or blank page; five minutes to aim the next twenty-five; questions to president and committee (why invite, why these people, issues, 1-year/3-year success, one thing to leave with).

**Agency hour:** after intros, the scripted-feeling-but-live agenda above. You look gutsy and useful; four identical competitors look like binders.

## Key Takeaways
1. Presenting ≠ selling; it is one late tool.
2. First meeting is never a presentation.
3. No discovery → no slide 4 → no right to pitch, even if asked.
4. Open decks with agenda, generic issues, then *their* situation; stop and talk.
5. If they block pre-meetings, steal the first 15 minutes for discovery and customize live.
6. Breaking pitch theater is safer than matching four lookalike monologues.
7. Own the process regardless of who owns the relationship.
